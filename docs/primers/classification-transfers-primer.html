Classification & Transfers 0 / 13

Issue #7 + PR #20 · ATherkel/budget

Is that 4,000 kr income, or just your own money moving?

Issue #7 and PR #20 decide how every transaction gets its household meaning — a category, a transfer, or an honest unknown. This page walks through it in thirteen short modules, each with a worked example in kroner. Tick a module off once you can explain it to someone else. Your progress is saved.

About this file

This is a primer, not a contract. It explains what docs/architecture/classification.md, ADR-011, ADR-012 and the Gold contract decide. Where they disagree with this page, they win.

Its companion, overlapping-exports-primer.html, covers which rows are the same transaction; this one covers what each transaction means.

The short answer

ADR-011 and ADR-012 are Accepted. You need five ideas — modules 02, 03, 05, 06 and 07 — and module 13 lists the five choices that were confirmed. Everything in between is what happens at the edges: competing legs, missing partners, rulings you make by hand, and what lands in front of you after a build.

Budget about 25 minutes. If you only have 10, read 02, 03, 05, 07 and 13.

Contents

01

The one question behind all of it

After this: you can state what issue #7 exists to decide, in one sentence.

The import side hands you clean, de-duplicated rows: an account, a date, an amount, some text. Every one of them still needs a household meaning — income, expense, refund, transfer, adjustment or unknown, plus a category where the type needs one.

Two of those meanings are the ones you must not confuse.

One row, two readings. joint-current, 1 February 2026.
DateTextAmountRead asWhat your year looks like
2026-02-01FROM BO+4,000.00income · contributionHousehold income is 48,000 kr a year higher, and it covers the joint spending it funds.
2026-02-01FROM BO+4,000.00transferIncome never sees it. The spending it funds is still an expense, so the household looks 48,000 kr a year worse at saving.

Four different things could supply that meaning: a rule you wrote, a ruling you made by hand, an opposite leg found on another one of your accounts, and the bank’s own category label. They disagree regularly. Issue #7 puts them in an order, and says what happens when even that is not enough.

The one sentence

For each transaction, which household meaning does the evidence actually support — and what happens when it supports none?

02

Why a wrong transfer is the dangerous kind of wrong

After this: you can say why the whole policy prefers a visible gap to a good guess.

Most classification mistakes are loud. File a supermarket run in the wrong category and the category looks wrong, so you notice. One mistake is silent.

Two unrelated transactions, two days apart, same amount.
AccountDateTextAmount
joint-current2026-03-10IKEA 551−250.00
anna-current2026-03-12MOBILEPAY CARL+250.00

A tempting reading: the amounts cancel, they are close in time, so this is 250 kr moving between your own accounts. Call it a transfer and both rows leave the income and expense totals entirely.

Paired as a transferLeft as two transactions
March expenses250.00 too lowcorrect
March income250.00 too lowcorrect
March savingexactly rightexactly right
Balancesunchangedunchanged
What the dashboard showsnothing at all—

That is the trap. A false transfer hides one income and one expense of the same size, so the saving figure — the number you would use to catch the error — comes out perfect. Nothing anywhere says a word.

The opposite failure is much milder. If Gold refuses to guess, each row is classified by its own rule, or stays unknown. An unknown counts toward unclassified money and raises a review item, so it turns up in front of you with the account, date, amount and text. That is issue #4’s unclassified total doing its job: unexplained money is a visible claim, not an artefact.

The principle, and its price

A visible unknown beats a silent guess. When the evidence is weak, conflicting or ambiguous, the transaction stays unknown.

The price is a longer review list. Modules 06 to 09 are mostly about keeping that list short enough to actually read — and the two rows above are not even candidates for pairing, so they cost you nothing at all (module 07).

03

What is inside the boundary — and what Bo’s money is

After this: you can say which accounts can hold a transfer, and what that costs you elsewhere.

A transfer is money moving inside the reporting boundary, so the boundary settles the question in module 01. Issue #6 defined it as every account the household or one of its members owns. Issue #7 narrows that to the accounts you actually import.

Bo is a household member. He sends 4,000.00 from his own bank to joint-current every month, and his account is never imported. Under #7 his account is outside the boundary, so that 4,000.00 is income in a contribution category (scenario 15).

In the real exports this is the common case, not the corner case: most rows the bank labels as transfers have no counterpart among the imported accounts at all.

Why not keep his account inside the boundary?

Because then his contributions would never count as household income, while the joint spending they fund would still count as expenses. The household would look far worse at saving than it is — module 02’s silent error, running every month. It would also leave an account inside the boundary with no data and no coverage flag, which is invisible.

Two consequences to be willing to live with

  • Money you send to a savings account you don’t import is an expense. Scenario 14: 1,000.00 TO SAVINGS with no counterpart anywhere. You settle it with a classify decision into a category such as external-saving, and it reads as spending. Whether the savings measure should treat it differently is left to issue #12.
  • Importing an account later rewrites history. Scenario 19: import Bo’s account and his outgoing leg now matches the 4,000.00 on the same day. The pair beats the contribution rule, the money stops being income, and every report restates. Before Bo’s managed period starts, FROM BO stays a contribution.

Check this one

This sharpens a definition #6 had already proposed, and it is the decision that moves the most money in your real data. If you disagree with it, most of modules 07 to 09 changes shape.

04

Your taxonomy, and what the bank’s labels are worth

After this: you know what you have to write down yourself, and why the free labels are not used.

The platform ships no categories at all. You define the taxonomy in configuration, and it has exactly two levels: category group → category. Only a category is ever assigned to a transaction.

  • Every category has a direction, income or expense, and all categories in a group share it — so a group rollup can never add salary to rent.
  • Identifiers are yours and durable, such as groceries. Renaming one is a display change and nothing is reclassified.
  • transfer, adjustment and unknown are transaction types, not categories. You never file something under "transfer".
  • You also need categories for money that crosses the boundary without being ordinary pay or spending: a contribution from Bo, an external-saving for savings you do not import (module 03). Those can never be transfers.

The bank’s own labels

Every Danske row carries a two-level bank label, free of charge. Issue #7 keeps them as Silver provenance only: a rule may test them like any other source text, and nothing else uses them. They are never copied into Gold, and there is no fallback that turns a bank label into a category when no rule matches.

The reason is that they do not mean what you mean:

  • the bank files transfers under its own income and expense groups;
  • it files returned goods and reimbursed outlays under income;
  • roughly one row in ten carries no bank label at all.

As a fallback they would misfile transfers and refunds without anyone noticing — module 02’s failure again. As a separate Gold column they would put a bank-specific field into the consumer contract that a second bank would break.

You lose nothing by it. "Bank label Groceries → my groceries" is an ordinary rule; you give it a low priority so that anything more specific wins (scenario 3, module 06). And the review workflow shows you the bank label through lineage while you are deciding.

This also settles an open question the Gold contract had been carrying — "retain bank categories as a Gold attribution?" — and that entry is removed.

05

A rule gives a category; the sign gives the type

After this: you can predict what a rule does to a refund without looking anything up.

You write "text contains NETTO → groceries". That rule meets two very different rows: −640.00 when you shopped, and +120.00 when you took something back.

If rules named the transaction type as well as the category, every expense rule would need a twin for refunds — and the first twin you forget quietly misfiles a return as income. So a rule assigns only the category, and the type follows from the category’s direction and the sign of the amount:

Category directionAmountType

incomenegativerefund

Scenarios 1, 2 and 6 — one rule each, no refund rules anywhere.
AccountDateTextAmountResultEffect
joint-current2026-02-02NETTO 0412−640.00expense · groceriesrule r-netto
joint-current2026-02-12NETTO 0412+120.00refund · groceriessame rule. With the row above, groceries net to 520.00.
joint-savings2026-03-31INTEREST ADJ−3.50refund · interestan income category going backwards: income falls by 3.50

So a reimbursed outlay — a friend paying back their share of dinner — nets against eating-out by itself, and returned interest nets against interest. That is exactly issue #4’s refund semantics.

The other two things a rule can assign

  • A transfer claim: "this is one leg of a transfer." The claim on its own never makes a transfer — the leg still has to be paired (modules 07 and 09).
  • An adjustment, with a reason. The reason becomes the explanation the contract requires for an adjustment.

A rule never assigns unknown. Not matching anything does that.

What a rule may look at

  • account_id;
  • the description: contains, starts with, or a regular expression, always case-insensitive;
  • the amount: its sign, or an inclusive range;
  • an inclusive date range, for a rule that only applies to a period — an old landlord’s rent, say;
  • the bank’s category labels.

And nothing else. A rule never looks at another transaction. Relating two rows to each other is transfer matching’s job alone, which is what keeps every rule explainable on its own.

Check this one

Automatic refunds are the surprising half of this module: a positive amount in an expense category becomes a refund whether or not you were thinking about returns when you wrote the rule.

06

Priority, not file order

After this: you can settle a rule collision, and you know why it is loud by default.

MOBILEPAY NETTO for −85.00 matches two of your rules: "starts with MOBILEPAY → eating-out" and "contains NETTO → groceries". Something has to give.

OptionWhy not
First rule in the file winsA broad rule placed early silently shadows every narrower rule below it, so a collision can never surface. Module 02 rules that out.
Most specific rule wins"More specific" between two text patterns is hard to define and harder to explain when it surprises you.
Explicit integer priorityChosen. 0 by default. The highest-priority matching rules decide.

If the highest-priority matches all say the same thing, that is the answer. If they disagree, there is no rule result: the transaction is unknown with a rule-conflict review item that names the rules (scenario 4). You then settle it deliberately, one of two ways:

  • Promote the specific rule. Add "contains MOBILEPAY NETTO → groceries" at priority 10, and the conflict item disappears on the next build (scenario 5).
  • Demote a fallback. The bank-label rule from module 04 sits at priority −10, so it only ever decides rows that nothing else claims (scenario 3).

Check this one

This is a deliberate trade: overlapping rules conflict loudly until you set a priority, and each conflict is a row you have to come back to. The alternative is a broad rule quietly eating your narrow ones, which you would never see.

07

What counts as proof of a transfer

After this: you can look at two rows and say whether they are allowed to pair.

The exports carry no identifier for the other account. All you have is the amount, the date and the text — and a fixed transfer amount recurs month after month, so the amount alone is never enough. Given module 02, the bar is set high.

Candidates

Two booked transactions may pair only when all of these hold:

  • they are on different Gold accounts — after module 03, that means both inside the boundary;
  • their amounts cancel exactly: one negative, the other its positive counterpart;
  • their transaction dates are at most 3 days apart — enough for Friday → Monday;
  • neither is targeted by a manual decision (module 10);
  • at least one leg carries a transfer claim from a rule, even on the same day. One low-priority rule on the bank’s transfer label covers the real ones.

Matching then runs in stages by date gap — 0 days, then 1, then 2, then 3 — so a same-day partner is always found before a looser one.

A transfer that costs a fee — 1,000.00 out, 995.00 in — is never a candidate either, because the amounts do not cancel, and a pair decision does not apply to it. Each leg is classified on its own until splits exist (issue #47).

Confidence is a name, not a number

A numeric confidence score with a threshold was rejected: it is hard to explain, hard to audit, and with this little evidence any threshold would be arbitrary. Instead lineage records the evidence basis by name — same_day, date_gap, repeated_legs, manual_pair or one_sided — so every pair can be defended in words.

Check this one

A pair beats a rule result. Seeing both legs in your own accounts is stronger evidence than a text pattern, and without this order a broad rule — "anything the bank calls other income → contribution" — would swallow the incoming leg of every transfer. It can still surprise: the rule you wrote loses to a pair you did not notice forming. Module 12 shows you those pairs.

08

When two legs compete for one match

After this: you can tell a harmless tie from one worth stopping for.

Same amount, same day, more than one possible partner. Two situations look identical from a distance, and only one of them is safe to resolve automatically.

Scenario 11 — interchangeable legs.
AccountDateTextAmount
anna-current2026-03-02BUDGET JOINT−2,000.00
anna-current2026-03-02BUDGET JOINT−2,000.00
joint-current2026-03-02BUDGET FROM ANNA+2,000.00
joint-current2026-03-02BUDGET FROM ANNA+2,000.00

Which outgoing leg pairs with which incoming leg changes no total whatsoever. These are repeated transactions of one another — same account, date, amount and text, the term issue #5 already defines — so Gold pairs them in account order and records the basis repeated_legs. Any leftover leg stays available for a later stage.

Scenario 12 — a real ambiguity.
AccountDateTextAmount
joint-current2026-03-05TO SAVINGS−1,500.00
joint-current2026-03-05DENTIST 7−1,500.00
joint-savings2026-03-05FROM CURRENT+1,500.00

Here the two outgoing legs are not interchangeable: pick the dentist and a real 1,500.00 medical bill vanishes from your expenses. So Gold pairs nothing. All three rows are unknown, and the set raises one ambiguous-transfer review item.

You settle it with a pair decision on the two savings legs. On the next build the dentist row falls back to its own rule and becomes expense · health, and the item is gone.

What Gold will never do

Break a tie by nearest date, or by whichever leg it saw first. Both are guesses, and a wrong guess here is silent.

09

The legs that never find a partner

After this: you know the difference between "wait" and "decide", and what a month boundary does.

A rule claims a row as a transfer leg and nothing pairs with it. Publishing it as a transfer anyway would hide money leaving the boundary, so the leg is unknown with an unmatched-transfer item — and the item tells you which kind of problem it is.

One-sided transfers

Sometimes the other leg can never be in Gold — a transfer into joint-savings in December 2025, when that account’s imported history starts in January 2026 (scenario 16). That is a one-sided-transfer, and it comes only from a manual decision naming the counterpart account.

It applies only when the transaction date falls outside that account’s managed period. Inside the managed period the other leg ought to exist, so the decision does not apply: pair it, or fix the import.

Across a month boundary

A pair may span two months (scenario 9). Each leg stays in its own month for account activity, household income and expenses change in neither month, and the household’s summed month-end balance is lower by the amount in flight. That last one is not an error — the money really was in transit.

10

The three times you rule by hand

After this: you know every way to overrule the machine, and why none of them can drift.

The word is manual decision, not "override". Issue #5 already put that term in the glossary for a human ruling that settles an import problem, and #7 widens the same term to cover classifying a transaction: a recorded human ruling that classifies a transaction, settles a review item, or voids an import run. It targets transactions by their identity and never edits source data.

KindTargetsResultDoes not apply when
classifyone transactiona category, type derived as in module 05, or adjustmentthe target is missing, or the derived type cannot fit the sign
pairtwo transactionsboth transfer, sharing one group, with any date gapa target is missing, the amounts do not cancel, or both are on one account
one-sided-transferone transaction plus a counterpart accounttransfer with no group (module 09)the target is missing, or the date falls inside the counterpart’s managed period

What a decision points at

A decision targets the transaction_id from issue #5 — the identity that survives re-imports, overlapping exports and rebuilds by design. When the bank rewords a transaction’s text, Silver’s same transaction ruling keeps the existing identifier, so the decision keeps its target.

Rejected targetWhy
The Gold account_sequenceIt shifts the moment an earlier row arrives, so the decision lands on a different transaction.
The source row: file plus positionDiffers between two exports of the same purchase.
A text patternThat is just a rule, and rules already exist.

The rules around them

  • Each decision has a durable decision_id and a short reason.
  • A transaction carries at most one decision, and a pair counts for both of its legs.
  • To undo a wrong automatic pair, classify either leg. There is no separate "not a transfer" kind, because a decision on one leg already displaces the pair.
  • If the target disappears — the bank withdrew the transaction — the decision raises a decision-not-applicable item and is never moved to a similar transaction (scenario 17). That is the whole point of targeting an identity.
  • Nothing has a "resolved" flag. A decision removes the cause, and the review item is simply gone on the next build.
11

Fail the build, or raise a review item

After this: you can predict which of your mistakes stops everything and which waits for you.

Two very different kinds of wrong. The split is where the problem is visible.

Visible in the configuration alone → the build fails

Nothing is published, and the error lists every offending entry at once. You just edited the file, so being told immediately is the kindest thing it can do. Examples: a rule or decision naming a category that does not exist (scenario 18); duplicate identifiers; a group whose categories disagree about direction; a rule with no outcome, or several; an unparseable pattern; an adjustment or decision with no reason; two decisions on one transaction; a one-sided transfer whose counterpart is not a registered account; deleting a category something still references (T4).

This mirrors issue #5’s import rule: admit the whole thing, or none of it.

Depends on the data → a review item

Nothing stops. The affected transaction gets whatever the evidence supports, often unknown, and an item explains why. Items are derived afresh on every build, so an item exists exactly while its cause does. Its identifier derives from the kind plus the identifiers involved, so the same situation keeps the same item across rebuilds.

KindRaised whenSettled by
unclassifiedno rule matched at alla new rule, or a classify decision
rule-conflictthe highest-priority matching rules disagree (module 06)a priority, a narrower rule, or a decision
sign-mismatchthe winning category’s direction cannot fit the sign (module 05)a narrower rule, or a decision
ambiguous-transfera candidate set is neither one pair nor repeated legs (module 08)a pair or classify decision
unmatched-transfera transfer claim finds no pair (module 09)the next import, a pair, one-sided-transfer or classify decision, or a rule fix

These six are Gold’s classification items. Silver’s import review items from issue #5 stay a separate list.

12

What lands in front of you after a build

After this: you know what the review workflow owes you, and what it deliberately leaves alone.

Gold publishes the items through its privileged lineage interface. After each build you get:

  1. A summary. Transactions by classification source; unclassified money in, money out and count per account and month; open items by kind.
  2. Every open item, with what you need to decide it. Account, transaction date, amount, description, the bank’s label, the rules or candidate legs involved, and which decision kinds would settle it. You should not have to go looking in the exports.
  3. The pairs worth eyeballing. Every pair formed on a date gap, and every pair where a leg’s own rule says something other than "transfer". This is the net under module 07: a claimed leg that paired with a coincidence shows up here, and a classify decision on either leg undoes it.

This output contains real amounts, so it goes to your terminal or a private report and never to routine logs.

What it does not touch

Coverage and the provisional-period label are unaffected by any of this. Coverage is about whether a balance can be trusted, and an unclassified amount does not make a balance wrong. Classification completeness is already visible through the unclassified total and the items — the contract requires that every unknown transaction has an open item. Keeping the two measures apart keeps both honest.

So — what is actually being asked of you?

What the PR puts in the repository

  • ADR-011 (precedence: decision → pair → rules → unknown; priority with conflict detection; type derived from direction and sign; bank labels as provenance) and ADR-012 (strong evidence only; staged matching; ambiguity and missing legs stay unknown; boundary = imported accounts).   • Gold contract 0.2, amended in place — there is no 0.3 because nothing has shipped. The five choices, in the order they matter

  1. The boundary is the accounts you import. So Bo’s money is income, and money sent to a savings account you do not import is an expense. This sharpens #6 and it moves the most money in your real data. (module 03)
  2. Refunds are automatic from the sign. A positive amount in an expense category is a refund; a negative amount in an income category is too. No refund rules anywhere. (module 05)
  3. A transfer pair beats a rule result. The rule you wrote loses to a pair you did not notice forming, and module 12’s "pairs worth eyeballing" is the only thing that shows you. (modules 07, 12)
  4. Rules are equal by default, so collisions are loud. Overlapping rules produce review items until you set a priority. (module 06)
  5. Every pair needs a rule’s transfer claim, even on the same day. You write one rule on the bank’s transfer label; in return, a coincidence can never pass as a transfer. Changed in the #20 review: the draft let same-day pairs through with no claim. (module 07)

And one word change: "manual decision" replaces "override" everywhere, which widens a term issue #5 already had accepted (module 10).

Accepted, and what is left

PR #20 flips ADR-011 and ADR-012 to Accepted. What remains is to close issue #7 with a resolution comment and append the outcome to the map in #2.

To change something later: write a new ADR that supersedes the point — "the boundary should include Bo’s account", say. Each of the five above lists the modules it drags with it, so you can see the size of the change first.

What is deliberately not here: versioning of rules, decisions and the taxonomy, and reproducing an old report (issue #8); file formats, CLI command names and how review output is presented (issue #10); whether money sent to savings you do not import should count differently in the savings measure (issue #12).
